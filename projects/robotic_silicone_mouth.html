Why I built it

I kept running into the same problem with talking robots: the voice came through, but the face just sat there, dead. The words were there; the life wasn't. So I decided to design a robotic mouth that could actually feels alive.

What I ended up with is the lower half of a face (the mouth and the area around it) made of soft silicone and driven by nine servos. It isn't trying to be a whole head, giving me more room for experimentation. It's a small, honest piece of a face that can do three genuinely useful things: pull a real expression, move in real time when speaking (audio speaker in sync), and quietly mirror back whatever you're doing.

I wanted each of those three skills to stand on its own, so I split the build into small, separate programs (threads and/or independent scripts). One is the main control panel where I calibrate, design expressions, and feed in audio (calibration and speaking). Another is a webcam-only mode where the robot copies me in real time (Ekman emotion recognition). A couple of smaller tools exist just to let me see and tune the math. And a single Arduino sketch quietly drives all nine servos. The nice side effect of that split is that I can break one thing without breaking the whole machine.

How it's wired

At the core it's a simple conversation: Python on my computer talks to an Arduino over USB, and the Arduino talks to a PCA9685 PWM board, which powers the servos. Most of the servos are pointed at an angle and go. But the four at the corners of the lips work differently, I don't tell them an angle, I tell them a spot in space to reach. That one change is what lets the mouth go lopsided, which is where all the real expression lives.

One detail I'm proud of: I can send all nine servos a new position in a single command instead of nine separate ones. That turns a clumsy, jumpy motion into something that feels continuous. When the mouth goes from surprise to a frown, it flows rather than stutters, and that's almost entirely thanks to batching the commands.

The trick under the silicone

Each lip corner is built from a five-bar linkage, two little servo arms, two fixed pivots, and the silicone lip piece tying the tips together. It's deliberately over-constrained, which means there's no single angle that puts the lip where I want it. So instead of fighting that, I let the software do the hard part: I give it a target point and it works backwards to figure out which two servos need to twist, using nothing but the law of cosines, that means, I did the clasic inverse kinematics equations for a five-bar linkage, and it works beautifully.

The left and right sides each run their own little solver, and each can flip to an elbow-up or elbow-down pose independently. That's the part that makes a happy smile work at all, one corner climbs, the other stays low, and the face suddenly reads as something human instead of something mechanical.

Teaching it feelings

I built the expression library around Ekman's classic emotions (neutral, happiness, sadness, anger, fear, surprise, disgust, and that quiet one, contempt). Each one is just a saved set of servo angles plus the two lip-corner targets I calibrated by hand. Because the corner servos are always re-derived from those targets, the saved poses stay tiny and stay clean, even when I tweak the geometry later.

Then I made it possible to blend. Pick any two or three expressions, give them weights, and the mouth slides somewhere in between. So an "amused but a little annoyed" look is a real, reachable face — not a thing I have to fake. There's a little radar chart that shows the mix live, which is far more honest than trying to describe it in words.

Getting it to speak

This is my favorite part. Feed it any audio, and it listens the way a person would: first it transcribes what's being said, then it breaks the words down into their sounds, and from those sounds it picks the right mouth shape and the right moment. By the time the audio plays out, the mouth already knows exactly when to open, when to round, when to close.

The whole thing runs locally, so nothing about the speech ever leaves the machine. Playback happens on its own thread so the interface never freezes, and I add a small pre-roll so the audio and the movement land on the same beat. And if it can't figure out a sound, it just settles back to rest instead of glitching, the mouth never freezes mid-sentence.

Mirroring you back

Point a webcam at me and the robot starts to do what I'm doing. It reads my face, smooths the reading so it doesn't twitch, and maps whatever it sees onto the closest expression it knows. The moment I relax below a certain confidence, it eases itself back to neutral, the whole exchange feels less like a demo and more like a conversation, or that I like to think haha.

I kept this one as its own program, even down to a private copy of the kinematics. It's a little redundant, but it means the mirror mode can run anywhere, on any machine, and it never gets stuck waiting on the serial port while it's watching me.

The pieces that hold it together

A few small decisions do a lot of heavy lifting. The firmware is the last line of defense and quietly clamps any angle that strays out of range before it reaches a servo. A tiny bit of hysteresis stops the servos from buzzing over tiny, pointless changes during a blend. And because everything I tune lives in plain JSON files, the whole system can be backed up, diffed, and versioned just like the code that drives it.

Where it goes next

What this little mouth taught me is that you don't need a whole face to make something feel alive, you need a small, honest set of moves, chosen well. Nine servos, two solvers, and a local audio pipeline were enough to produce real expression, real timing, and a real sense of being mirrored back.

The next step is to make a pair of eyes that can do the same thing, and then a whole head that can do it all together. But for now, this little mouth is enough to make me smile.
