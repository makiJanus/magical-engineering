Emily: a fully autonomous social robot and development platform

▶ Watch it on my YouTube channel!

Why I built her

The mouth could talk. The eyes could look. But they were two separate toys, each with its own computer, its own code, its own idea of what "now" means. Now its time to integrate and evolve into a fully autonomous social robot.

So Emily is the project where I stopped assembling and started integrating. She takes the two things I had already built and makes them one robot. She listens, thinks, speaks, looks at you, blinks, and shows you what she's doing on a little screen, all as one continuous gesture.

The name is deliberate. I wanted her to feel like a character with a mind of her own, someone you can have a turn with, not a machine you operate. Everything in the design, from the push-to-talk button to the calm way she settles to rest, is in service of that single feeling: that you're talking to a being.

What she is

At her core Emily is one command: python emily.py. That single program is the whole robot. Around it are four cooperating computers: a Raspberry Pi 5 running the brain and body, an Arduino Nano driving the head, and two little ESP32 boards that each carry a screen with its own job.

Her head is 24 servos: twelve for the eyes (six per eye, pan, tilt, two eyelids, two brows), three for the neck so her head can follow you, and nine for the mouth so she can shape real sounds. The front screen is a round state mirror that says, in a single word, what Emily is doing (Listening, Thinking, Talking). The back screen is dull control deck: menus for demos, calibration, Wi-Fi, and self-diagnosis, on an encoder and a back button.

How she's wired

Under the hood it's the same family as the mouth and the eyes: Python on the Pi, an Arduino in the middle, and a pair of PCA9685 PWM boards translating pulses into servo positions. The head is one serial chain. Each display hangs off its own I2C address, 0x42 for the front, 0x43 for the back, and the whole thing shares one configuration file and one directory of JSON data.

The detail I'm proudest of is how cleanly the pieces stay out of each other's way. The head bus has a single owner at a time, the calibration files are shared on disk but only written on an explicit save, and every screen can simply be missing without breaking the voice loop. Emily degrades gracefully: lose the head and she keeps talking, lose a screen and she keeps her mind.

The head

The head is modeled as one shared pose: a flat vector of 24 angles. Every controller (eyes, neck, mouth) writes only its own slice, and the bus merges them into a single frame. That's the whole trick that keeps 24 servos from ever fighting over each other.

What about the lip sync? Her spoken reply text becomes a timed track of visemes, and the mouth animates against the audio as it plays, long vowels hold longer than consonants, the way a real mouth does. When she finishes, she settles the mouth to rest. This is the difference between a mouth that moves and a mouth that's talking.

The conversation brain

A turn with Emily goes like this: hold the button and she listens, release and she thinks, then she speaks ,and her face does everything a face should while she speaks. That last part is a real agent loop, not a canned response. She has personas I can hot-swap, tools she can call on her own, and a working memory for the session.

She's built to be swappable at every seam. The speech recognition, the synthesis, the LLM, each one is just configuration. Right now she runs on Deepgram for listening and speaking with an OpenAI-compatible endpoint for thinking, but the local fallbacks are all there (but not active), waiting in the code.

The behavior model is intentionally simple. She has a few states (initializing, listening, thinking, talking) and she owns exactly one of them at a time. And she's never always-listening: the microphone only listens while you hold the button, which is a deliberate choice about privacy and about what it means to be talked to.

Her two interfaces and how I tune her

The front screen is for everyone: one word over an animated gradient that says how she's feeling right now. The back screen is for me — a full operator plane with a menu map, a self-diagnosis page, and entry points to everything. Two displays, two roles, and neither one can take her down.

And because a face this expressive is only as good as its calibration, I built a browser tool where I can drive all 24 servos, tune the eye mirror, and shape the mouth's lip-corner anchors by hand, live-previewing a pose and only saving it when it's right.

Where it goes next

Emily is the face I'd been building toward all along, the eyes, the mouth, the brain, and the body finally agreeing on the same "being" The next step is to make that agreement tighter: fewer seams, smoother handoffs, and a memory that lasts past the session, so she starts to feel less like a platform and more like someone you've met before.
