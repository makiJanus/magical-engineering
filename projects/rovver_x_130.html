Rovver X 130: Digital Twin

The Problem

Municipal pipe inspection involves difficult environments, expensive equipment, trained personnel, and strict safety requirements. Inspection robots reduce the need to place people inside pipes while providing visual and dimensional information about the infrastructure.

I researched this field to understand how inspection robots are designed and operated. I then used the available public information to build a digital twin of the ROVVER X 130 and explore its behavior in simulated pipe environments.

Looking Into the Rovver X 130

According to Envirosight's public specifications, the ROVVER X 130 is a teleoperated sewer-inspection system for underground environments. It can travel up to 1,640 feet, uses six-wheel drive, and is designed for pipes between 6 and 96 inches in diameter. These values describe the manufacturer's system; the digital twin is an approximation based on the available references.

The platform uses interchangeable wheels for different pipe conditions. Its camera can tilt and rotate and provides a wide field of view with 10x optical and 12x digital zoom. Envirosight also documents inspection-catalog integrations such as PACP, MACP, and LACP; those systems support recording observations and generating reports, but are outside the scope of this digital twin.

The system is used for municipal sewer and stormwater inspection and can also support inspections in industrial environments. The exact deployment requirements depend on the pipe geometry, terrain, and inspection procedure.

Digital Twin

Envirosight publishes dimensions for the system, but the reference material does not always identify which component each measurement describes. I used a flat reference image in Fusion 360, scaled it against the available dimensions, and built the CAD model from the resulting proportions. This produces a visual approximation rather than verified production geometry.

After completing the CAD model, I import it into Onshape to configure the joints. I then add the robot's sensors and lights in Isaac Sim, export the assembled scene as USD, and prepare it for simulation tests.

Results

To evaluate the digital models, I created test circuits that approximate real pipe shapes and appearances. These scenes test whether the reconstructed robot and its accessories can traverse the simulated environments; they do not yet measure real-world traction, sensor fidelity, or inspection accuracy.

Simulation can reveal clearance problems, unsuitable accessory configurations, and control issues before a physical prototype is built. The current tests therefore reduce early design risk, while physical validation remains necessary before making claims about field performance.
