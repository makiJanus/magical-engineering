Why I built it

The mouth I built before this one could speak, but a talking robot with no eyes still felt a little uncanny. So this is the other half of the face: a pair of modular silicone eyes that can actually look at you.

These are not just two servos per eye. Each eye has six of them for pan, tilt, an upper and lower eyelid, and an inner and outer brow, so that's twelve servos working together. That's what lets them do more than glance: they can blink, frown, look suspicious, sleep, or just wander around while holding an expression. The whole point is that the eyes feel awake.

Everything is built around one idea: calibration is the single source of truth. Every neutral angle, every fine trim, and every rule for how the left eye mirrors the right lives in one JSON file, and every part of the software reads it at startup. That means the control interface, the face tracker, and the wander demo all agree with each other without any of them needing to talk to each other.

How it's wired

Under the hood it's the same family as the mouth: Python on the computer, an Arduino in the middle, and a PCA9685 PWM board powering the servos. The right eye takes channels 0–5 and the left takes 8–13. The Arduino is really just a thin serial server, it turns an angle into a pulse and hands it to the board, nothing more.

One thing I kept from the mouth and doubled down on is the batched command. I can send all twelve servos a new position in a single line, and that's what keeps the loop fast enough to feel real-time. One slider change is one write, not twelve, and that's the whole reason the gaze stays smooth instead of jerky.

The trick: one eye drives the other

This is the part I'm proudest of. I never command the left eye directly. I command the right, and the left is derived from it, each axis gets a flip and a gain, and it all pivots on that eye's own neutral. So if I turn my right eye a certain amount, the left turns the same amount the other way, scaled to whatever I've tuned.

Because the mirror pivots on each side's own neutral, I can move the rest position around in calibration and the mirroring keeps working. That's why the calibration tab and the live-control tab are completely independent, and that's exactly on purpose, because it means I can tune the shape of the gaze without breaking where the eyes rest.

Eyelids, blinks, and feeling

An eye that can't blink is just a camera. So the eyelids get their own care. I stored a handful of expressions (neutral, happy, sad, angry, surprised, suspicious, and sleepy) and each one is a full twelve-angle pose. The sleepy one is my favorite: the lids sit low and the whole thing reads as tired instead of mechanical.

On top of the expressions I built a small blink machine: close, hold, open, and the occasional double blink. It's deliberately simple, but it's the difference between an eye that feels alive and one that's just pointing at things.

Making them move

Expressions are still poses. To make the eyes feel alive on their own, I wrote a set of motion patterns (quick saccades, smooth tracking, a slow circle, a dizzy spiral, and the four look directions). Each one runs on its own thread and can be mirrored to both eyes or kept on the right. Those are very useful for calibration purposes, when we can see on real time how the eyes coordinate on diffrent scenarios.

Following you

Point a webcam at me and the eyes follow my face. It's not fancier than it needs to be, a classic face detector each frame, a little smoothing so the gaze doesn't twitch, and the blink machine layered on top. When I step out of frame for long enough, they ease themselves back to neutral, as if they've given up on me and gone to rest.

I like that it uses the exact same controller and the same mirror math as the interface. There's one definition of how the eyes move, and every mode just feeds it a different target.

On their own

The wander demo is my playground. Every tick it blends three independent engines into a single pose: one that wanders the gaze around, one that slowly eases into a new expression, and one that handles the blinks. The blinks only ever add closure (they never force the lids open past where the expression already put them) so a sleepy face doesn't accidentally snap wide open.

Where it goes next

Put the eyes and the mouth together and you have a face. That's where I want this to go next, the eyes and the mouth sharing the same sense of 'now', so the gaze, the blink, and the words all land as one gesture instead of three separate ones.
