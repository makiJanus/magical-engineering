POWER SYSTEMS / 2020

Make every 3.3V device rechargeable

A compact load-sharing circuit that charges a 3.7 V LiPo battery and delivers a regulated 3.3 V supply while the device is in use.

Download the design files

Design goals

The circuit was designed around three requirements:

  1. Charge a 3.7 V Li-Ion/Li-Po battery from USB, with a maximum charge voltage of 4.2 V.
  2. Keep the device running while the battery is charging.
  3. Provide a regulated 3.3 V output for the device.

The final schematic is shown below. It has two main jobs: managing the battery charge and selecting the correct power source for the load.

Charging the battery

The MCP73838 is the heart of the charging section. It is a dedicated battery-charge controller that needs only a small number of external components. The reference design from its datasheet is shown below:

Because this version is powered through USB, the AC-related parts of the reference design, including the common-cathode dual diode and the VC connection, are not used. Pins 3 and 4 report the charging state and fault conditions through the STAT1 and STAT2 outputs:

Standby High Resistance HighResistance

Charge Complete High Resistance LOW
Temperature Fault High Resistance High Resistance

The LED indicators are not completely unambiguous: both standby and a temperature fault produce the same output state. In practice, standby is easy to identify because it occurs when no battery is connected. In this design, STAT2 drives a green LED and STAT1 drives a blue LED.

Setting the charge current

The TE pin is pulled low to enable the built-in safety timer, which places the charger in standby after four, six, or eight hours depending on the device variant. The AC-related control pin is also pulled low because those functions are not used. PROG1 sets the battery-charge current through an external resistor. Here, a 2 kOhm resistor sets the current to 500 mA, or approximately 0.5C for the selected battery. The datasheet gives the following relationship:

IREG = 1000V/RPROG

Load sharing

The second part of the circuit manages the transition between USB power and the battery. When USB is connected, VBUS supplies the load. When USB is removed, the battery takes over automatically. The following application-note diagrams explain the intended power path:

This power path is important. If the load is connected directly to the battery, the charger can interpret the device current as part of the charging current and fail to terminate charging correctly. The MCP73838 ends a charge cycle when the current falls below a defined threshold, so the load must be isolated from the battery-charge path.

A PMOS-based circuit provides that isolation while allowing the battery to power the device when USB is absent:

With USB disconnected, resistor R5 pulls the PMOS gate to 0 V and turns the transistor on, allowing the battery to power the load. The transistor is not ideal: its dropout voltage is up to 1.1 V, typically about 750 mV. This means the regulator input can fall to approximately 3.6 V in the worst case, or about 3.95 V under typical conditions. When USB supplies 5 V, the PMOS turns off and the load is powered from VBUS through the diode. With a maximum forward drop of 520 mV, the regulator input is approximately 4.48 V. The diode also prevents current from flowing back toward the USB input or the MOSFET gate. Its worst-case leakage current is 50 uA, which creates about 500 mV across R5, safely below the level that would turn the PMOS off. Finally, the 3.3 V regulator has a maximum dropout voltage of 400 mV, so its input should remain above 3.7 V. Under typical conditions, the dropout is closer to 250 mV, corresponding to an input of about 3.55 V. The regulator is rated for a maximum load of 600 mA.

Validation: a rechargeable wireless mouse

To test the design, I converted an inexpensive 2.4 MHz wireless mouse from disposable AAA batteries to a rechargeable battery. I could not identify the mouse's internal IC, but because the original supply was 3 V, I expected a regulated 3.3 V input to be suitable. I removed the battery holder and installed the charger, regulator, and new battery in its place.

The mouse draws approximately 25 mA at 3.0 V, and the battery has a capacity of 250 mAh. Ignoring conversion losses and variation in current consumption, that gives an ideal runtime of about 10 hours per charge. A future revision could add a dedicated opening for the USB connector or a redesigned 3D-printed enclosure.

The conversion demonstrates that the circuit can replace disposable batteries in a low-power device while preserving normal operation. The electrical design worked in the original enclosure; the remaining improvements are mainly mechanical, especially making the charging port easier to access.
