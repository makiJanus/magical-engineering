Mini Vizca 400: Digital Twin

The Problem

Maquintel Robotic Services develops mobile robots for inspecting pipes and sluice channels. I wanted to understand how these robots are designed, but I do not have direct access to them, so this project reconstructs a digital twin from publicly available information.

In this project, I designed a digital twin by analyzing the company's public images, videos, and published dimensions.

Maquintel's Robots Overview

Maquintel presents five robots that can be grouped into two broad categories: tracked platforms and wheeled platforms. They address similar inspection tasks, but their locomotion systems suit different environments.

The images suggest that the robots within each class share a common design. For tracked platforms, the ORG-7 appears to be the base configuration; combining two units with a camera produces the ORG-S7, while placing two units below a camera produces the ORG-DP7. Among the wheeled platforms, the Vizca D400 and Mini Vizca 400 appear to share the same general layout, with differences in size and capacity. These relationships are visual inferences, not verified internal designs.

This case study focuses on the Mini Vizca 400 as a representative wheeled platform. The reconstruction is based only on information shared through Maquintel's website and social media. Its intended use is inspecting tunnels and sluice channels, measuring wear with lidar and profilometers.

The available images suggest a configurable sensor layout. A lidar-like sensor appears at the front in some configurations and may also be mounted at the rear. The profilometer is shown on one side, while two or three cameras and lights are generally mounted at the upper front. Because these observations come from images, each position should be treated as an assumption for the digital twin.

The changing number and position of these elements suggest a modular platform. For the virtual replica, I organize the robot into four conceptual modules: locomotion, vision, sensors, and structure.

  1. Locomotion: 4 motors + encoders + wheels.
  2. Vision: two side cameras, two lights, and one front camera without a dedicated light.
  3. Sensors: Lidar, IMU.
  4. Structure: an aluminum chassis with modular connectors, a rear emergency button, and four support wheels to guide the robot.

Mini Vizca 400 Digital Twin

Maquintel provides approximate dimensions rather than a complete technical drawing. If at least one reference dimension is correct, the remaining dimensions can be estimated by preserving the visible proportions and measuring the model in Fusion 360.

After completing the CAD model, I import it into Onshape to configure the joints. I then add the sensors and lights in Isaac Sim, configure their parameters, export the scene as USD, and use it for simulation tests.

Project Outcomes

A lidar emits laser pulses and estimates distance from their return time. As the sensor rotates, those measurements form a two-dimensional scan around the robot. Combining successive scans with the robot's estimated pose produces a point cloud that represents the surrounding space.

The resulting point cloud is a useful approximation of the inspection workflow. A wear estimate could be produced by comparing the measured profile with a reference geometry, but this project simulates the pipeline and does not validate Maquintel's internal measurement process.
